What makes a haiku

A haiku is a three-line poem that holds one moment. In English it is usually written as 5, 7 and 5 syllables. The form comes from Japan, where poets counted sound units rather than syllables and often included a word that placed the poem in a season.

The count is the easy part to recognise. The harder part is what happens inside it:

  • One concrete image. Something you could see, hear or touch, not an idea about it.
  • The present tense. The moment is happening now.
  • A cut. A small turn, usually before the last line, where the poem steps sideways and the two halves throw light on each other.
  • No rhyme, no title. The poem carries itself.

How Poem Writer keeps the form

Ask a language model for a haiku and it will often hand back four lines, or a rhyming couplet with a line on top. Poem Writer doesn’t show those as haiku. Before a haiku reaches you, the app checks it:

  • exactly three lines, in one stanza;
  • 5, 7 and 5 syllables, allowing one syllable either way, because syllables in English are counted by rule of thumb and words like “fire” and “every” can go either way;
  • a concrete image in the present tense, with the turn between the second and third line, and no rhyme.

If a line is off, the app sends the problem back to be rewritten, so the poem you read is already in shape. Syllables are counted for English haiku. In the other languages the app writes in, it keeps the three lines and the single stanza.
